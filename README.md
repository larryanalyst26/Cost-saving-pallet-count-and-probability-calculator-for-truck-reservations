# Cost-saving-pallet-count-and-probability-calculator-for-truck-reservations

This is a program I created and used in my previous dispatching job, coded in Python, to find the most cost efficient truck size reservations for each city we shipped to. This project was not asked of me. I did it on my own accord. I saved the company 5-15% daily in truck costs by using this program.

The technical problem I was solving:

The company I worked for was the shipping company for Temu and Shein. Our warehouse had to send all our daily packages out to the 12 cities we shipped to. The packages are sorted by city into pallets (large cardboard boxes) that held 400-800 packages each. The pallets were shipped by truck. There were two truck sizes, 53 feet and 26 feet. 53 footer held 30 pallets, and the 26 held 13. The 53 always had a big discount relative to capacity. Every city had their own price and the 26 to 53 cost ratio also differed, but for purposes of describing the problem we will use a plausible example and have the 26 footer be $600 and the 53 be $750. Pallets were constructed and made during the day and the shipping began at night. All packages must be sent out every night. 53's had to be reserved no less than 2 hours ahead of shipping time. 26's could be reserved at any time. Also, within 2 hours of shipping, trucks could not be cancelled. We did not know how many pallets each city would have until shipping time began, so we had to reserve all big trucks before we knew how many pallets there would be for each city. This presented a unique technical challenge in trying to minimize truck shipping costs. The daily warehouse package volume was usually known early in the day. Prior to me creating this program, the dispatchers were just eyeballing it the best they can from experience. But they had to always play it safe because getting too much truck ahead of time was a much smaller loss than not getting enough and having to add a 26'. I will elaborate on this, and what my program does is allow us to not play it safe but play it best. The technical challenge is that it is very common for a city, like the one we are using for example with $600/$750 truck costs, to be expected to be in the pallet range of around 43 pallets, maybe 40 to 46 (from eyeballing based on total warehouse package count). If you reserve a 53' and a 26', it will cost $1350 in this case. If the pallets end up being less than or equal to 43, then they could fit and you'd save $150 compared to two 53 footers which is $1500. But if the pallets go over 43, they would not fit and you would need to add a 26'. You can no longer add a 53' (even if you did not already order a 26', as it is too late for the 53' once you know the final pallet count). The price of a 53' and two 26' is $1950. So you'd lose $450 compared to if you just played it safe and got two 53' (which is $1500). This program solves this dilemma. As I stated, the dispatchers before I made this program always would just get two 53' in these scenarios which occurred daily. You cannot come close to optimally solving this by eye. The respective share of each city toward the total warehouse package count fluctuates. Therefore the expected pallet count of each city given the total warehouse package count fluctuates. We do not know the package count of any city by truck reserving time, only the total warehouse volume. Every city also has different variances in their respective share fluctuation. What my program does is model the true probability that a city will have less than or equal to a threshold number of pallets, in the case given it would be 43, given the total warehouse package count and previous days' data. With the true, or as close to true as possible, probability, you can know the most long term cost efficient truck combination to order as you know the long term win loss. 

The mathematical/statistical technique I used:

First, I used weighted linear regression using total warehouse package count as the independent variable and the corresponding pallet count of a particular shipping city as the dependent variable. The linear logic behind the model is basically saying that given a total warehouse package count, it would have a linear factor predictive correlation for any particular city's pallet count. This logic is backed by historical inspection of warehouse data. The key aspect being that large warehouse total volume means increased pallet count for all cities, confirmed historically. Every city's linear factor would of course vary because each city has varying significance in its usual share of the total warehouse volume. Typically, more populated cities contribute more, which makes sense. I always verified in my graph that the residuals are homoskedastic (constant variance at all input points) with mean of 0 (visually looked like) at each input point. There does not seem to be any dependence between days and thus the assumptions of linear regression are met. Since things could change between cities, as some cities grew in their expected respective share as the business expanded in that city, I used only the previous 2 weeks of data. Going too far back could include data points that were including information that was no longer relevant (a city had changed in its relative share). Also, since the business was steadily growing, I used weighted linear regression by placing more weight on the more recent data points. So I used a simple sliding weight scale by putting a weight of 14 on yesterday's data and decreasing the weight by 1 going back each day until I reached 14 days ago which was the last day I used with a weight of 1. The weight is the equivalent of using that day's data point that many times. So like repeating yesterday's data 14 times and the day before that 13 times, etc. This 2 week range seemed to work the best because it used enough data to capture the full spectrum of big days and little days but avoided going too far back and capturing data that was no longer relevant. I then feed the total package count for the current day into the program into this regression with the previous 14 days of pallet counts for the city I am calculating and the corresponding total warehouse package counts of those days and the regression gives me the prediction, which is the mean pallet count for the shipping city with the current day's package total. Of course there is a fluctuation, and we want to know the probability in the scenario I gave where maybe the mean was 41.9. As I noted earlier, each city could be different in its volatility in pallet count, given a particular total warehouse volume. I would then calculate the probability the pallet count would be less than or equal to 43. In the pricing scenario I gave, it needs to be <= 43 more than 3 times as often as it is >43 for me to reserve a 53 and a 26 and win long term. That would be a threshold probability of 75%. To calculate this probability, I use prediction intervals. What this is, is calculating two separate, independent, and therefore summative, variances that the pallet count has. One is the variance of the prediction given the model. If the model predicts a mean of 41.9 with variance of W, that is one variance. There however is additional variance. The prediction line we calculated itself has variance. The variance has to do with the data, how many datapoints, etc. This variance is independent of the variance the model has for the prediction itself, so they add up. Both of these variances are of a normal distribution. So with these two normal distributions, we can determine the probability, or where on the final curve with both sources of variance, our threshold point of 43 lies. We then have the probability it will be less than or equal to 43, and we can then make the most long term cost effective truck reservations. If the probability that the particular city will have over 43 pallets is 75.1%, then we must reserve 2 53's. If it is 74.9%, we'd reserve a 53 and a 26, and if it did go over, we'd add a 26. Everyday, we retrain the model by shifting the 14 day data range to include the most recent day and exclude the least recent day that was previously used the day before.

Description of the predPalletsPerCity function itself:

The function takes 5 inputs. wtsl is the list of weights to use in the weighted linear regression for each day of data, from the most recent date to the least recent. xl is the list of independent inputs, which is the previous days' total warehouse package count, from most recent to least recent. yl is the list of dependent inputs, which is the corresponding pallet counts for the specific shipping city over the same date range, in the same order. packages is the current day's total warehouse package count to be shipped. Threshold is the threshold I want to calculate there will be less than or equal to that amount of pallets, which would be 43 in the previous example. If you wanted to calculate the probability of it being greater than a number, which I rarely needed to in the scope I was operating in, you would just subtract the probability you got from 1. This program is robust, if you wanted to change the number of days, or the corresponding weights, you just changed it how you wanted to in the inputs. First, the program graphs the training data on the generated model, so that I can make sure the assumptions of linear regression are met. The program then returns first the mean prediction then a comma and then the probability it will be less than or equal to the given threshold. The math in the program is the math to calculate weighted linear regression without literally using the previous day's data 14 times and then the day before that 13 times, etc. It avoids having to actually be that repetitive by using mathematical shortcuts. It also includes the math to calculate the prediction interval to get the probability for the threshold.
