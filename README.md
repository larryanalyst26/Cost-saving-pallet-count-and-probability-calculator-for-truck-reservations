# Cost-saving-pallet-count-and-probability-calculator-for-truck-reservations
This is a program I created in my previous dispatching job, coded in Python, to find the most cost efficient truck size reservations for each city we shipped to. I want to note that this was done entirely by my own accord and was not asked of me. I spent time on this that was not paid extra for, so there are probably aspects where I could have went more in depth and definitely would have had I been in a technical role, which this was not.

The technical problem I was solving:

Our warehouse received packages daily and sent packages to the same group of cities each day. These packages were just things consumers ordered (off either Temu or Shein) and was being shipped to them for delivery. Obviously there will be some fluctuation both in total package count and in share of packages sent to each city, but there is definitely a linear correlation which is what I use to solve this problem. Also, different cities could have different variances in the respective shares. Some were more volatile. Those variance could also change. Packages were sorted into pallets to be shipped to each city. Pallets are large cardboard boxes that held anywhere from 400 to 800 packages each. Some pallets were relatively empty with perhaps 200 packages. Truck costs varied for each city. There was a 26 foot truck and a 53 foot truck. The 26 held 13 pallets easily, but could also hold 14 if loaded with the proper technique and if the pallets weren't all huge, in which case you could only fit 13 even with loading skill. The 53 maxed out at 30 pretty much every time. The truck costs however were not proportional to their capacity. The 53 had a significant discount for all cities. The cost difference and ratios between the sizes varied for each city. I will give an example that is a very reasonable scenario, being that the 26 costed $600 and the 53 costed $750. However, 53's could only be reserved with at least a couple hours in advance. 26's could be added at any time, even during shipping time. Also, we were not to cancel trucks within 2 hours of shipping time. Shipping time begins with all pallets to be shipped are constructed. So in other words, we do not know how many pallets are to be shipped to each city by the time we must finalize truck reservations. We had to go off how many total packages for all cities combined there were. We did not know how many packages each city had by the time trucks had to be finalized. Before I wrote this function and started using it to reserve trucks, the other dispatchers were just eyeballing it and had to always be extra safe and get more trucks or bigger trucks than they needed, because it was absolutely not allowed to ever not ship all pallets each night. There were about 10 cities that we shipped to, and although you could have a general idea of how many pallets there would be with the total warehouse package count. Eyeballing it mad it impossible to save money in the scenarios, which happen everyday, where for example a city would probably have somewhere between 38 and 46 pallets. By eye, you can't just decide to reserve a 53 and a 26, because you don't know with precision that it would be 44 pallets or less. If it went over, you could only add another 26 and have 2 26's and a 53, which is war less efficient than 2 53's. So there policy before I wrote this function was to rather be wrong by getting more than they needed because it wasted less money to get 2 53's when they could have gotten a 53 and a 26 ($150 to be exact in the scenario I gave) than was wasted by trying to get a 53 and a 26 to save and then be wrong and have to add another 26 at shipping time when you could have just played safe and gotten 2 53's to start. The basic game we are trying to win at here is trying to take advantage of the $150 discount by getting a 53 and a 26 (versus 2 53's) and being able to fit the packages on them (with only the total combined packages to be shipped for all 10 cities) and doing this successfully enough times than we profit when subtracting the times we are wrong and have to add a 26 at the last moment and basically have. 53 and 2 26's, which in the pricing scenario I gave costs $450 than just playing it safe and getting 2 53's. So we need to be right, in this scenario, by more than 3 to 1 when compared to how often we are wrong, to add value. What my function does, is zero in on the true probability that in this scenario that particular city would have less than or equal to 43 pallets (be able to fit on a 53 and a 26).

The mathematical/statistical technique I used:

First, I used weighted linear regression using total warehouse package count as the independent variable and the corresponding pallet count of a particular shipping city as the dependent variable. Since things could change between cities, as some cities grew in package count as the business expanded in that city, I used only the previous 2 weeks of data. Going too far back could include data points that were including information that was no longer relevant (a city had changed in its relative share). Also, since the business was steadily growing, I used weighted linear regression by placing more weight on the more recent data points. So I used a simple sliding weight scale by putting a weight of 14 on yesterday's data and decreasing the weight by 1 going back each day until I reached 14 days ago which was the last day I used with a weight of 1. The weight is the equivalent of using that day's data that many times. So like repeating yesterday's data 14 times and the day before than 13 times, etc. This 2 week range seemed to work the best because it used enough data to capture the full spectrum of big days and little days but avoided going too far back and capturing data that was no longer relevant. I then feed the total package count for the current day into the program into this regression with the previous 14 days of data for the city I am calculating and the corresponding total package counts of those days and the regression gives me the prediction, which is the mean pallet count for the shipping city with the current day's package total. Of course there is a fluctuation, and we want to know the probability in the scenario I gave where maybe the mean was 41.9. As I noted earlier, each city could be different in its volatility in pallet count, given a particular total warehouse volume. I would then calculate the probability the pallet count would be less than or equal to 43. In the pricing scenario I gave, it needs to be <= 43 more than 3 times as often as it is >43 for me to reserve a 53 and a 26 and win long term. That would be a threshold probability of 75%. To calculate this probability, I use what is known as prediction intervals. What this is, is calculating two separate, independent, and therefore summative, variances that the pallet count has. One is the variance of the prediction. If the model predicts a mean of 41.9 with variance of W, that is one variance. There however is additional variance which can be easily missed. The prediction line we calculated itself has variance. The variance has to do with the data, how many datapoints, etc. This variance is independent of the variance the line calculates for the prediction, so they add up. Both of these variances are of a normal distribution. So with these two normal distributions, we can determine the probability, or where on the final curve with both sources of variance, our threshold point of 43 lies. We then have the probability it will be less than or equal to 43, and we can then make the most long term cost effective truck reservations. If the probability that the particular city will have over 43 pallets is 76%, then we must reserve 2 53's. If it is 74%, we'd reserve a 53 and a 26, and if it did go over, we'd add a 26. Everyday, we retrain the model by shifting the 14 day data range to include the most recent day and exclude the least recent day that was previously used the day before.

Description of the function itself:

The function takes 5 inputs. wtsl is the list of weights to use in the weighted linear regression for each day of data, from the most recent date to the least recent. xl is the list of independent inputs, which is the previous days' total warehouse package count, from most recent to least recent. yl is the list of dependent inputs, which is the corresponding pallet counts for the specific shipping city over the same date range, in the same order. packages is the current day's total warehouse package count to be shipped. Threshold is the threshold I want to calculate there will be less than or equal to that amount of pallets, which would be 43 in the previous example. If you wanted to calculate the probability of it being greater than a number, which I rarely needed to in the scope I was operating in, you would just subtract the probability you got from 1. This program is robust, if you wanted to change the number of days, or the corresponding weights, you just changed it how you wanted to in the inputs. The program then returns first the mean prediction then a comma and then the probability it will be less than or equal to the given threshold. The math in the program is the math to calculate weighted linear regression without literally using the previous day's data 14 times and then the day before that 13 times, etc. It avoids having to actually be that repetitive by using mathematical shortcuts. It also includes the math to calculate the prediction interval to get the probability for the threshold.
